# Reflection
Looking back at the past ten days, what has struck me most is how quickly multimodal, tool-using models have moved from demos to infrastructure. In this very homework, I extracted structured amounts from seven receipt images by writing a short LangChain prompt — a task that would have required a dedicated OCR pipeline and several hundred lines of parsing code just two years ago.

This course means a lot to me personally. Before taking it, I had already used AI for "vibe coding" — I could get small website demos up and running, but I never really understood why they worked or how to make them reliable. It felt like a magic trick I didn't control. SEEM5660 changed that: it gave me a real mental model of LLM programming — how a chain is structured, what the model is responsible for versus what should be computed deterministically, how prompts become products, and how to validate outputs instead of trusting them. The difference between "making a demo work once" and "building something that works on unseen data" is exactly what I learned here, and it is the most valuable part of the course for me.

For my career in FinTech, this shifts where the value lies. Writing a prompt that gets a right answer once is easy; designing a chain that is robust across unseen receipts — deciding what to ask the model versus what to compute deterministically, validating JSON outputs, handling edge cases — is the real skill. My plan has shifted from "learn every model architecture" to "learn to design reliable agentic systems that combine LLM judgment with deterministic verification." The competitive advantage won't be prompting tricks; it will be the discipline of treating model outputs as untrusted data and wrapping them in code that catches and corrects errors.
